# Excellent Writing

Make the writing worth the reader's attention, then make its argument easy to follow. Work from the whole piece down to the sentence; elegant wording cannot repair an irrelevant problem or unsupported claim.

Adapted from Writer Science's *Anatomy of a Great Essay: A Visual Guide to Expert Writing*, supplied as `anatomy of a great-essay.pdf` (four pages). Page references below identify the source principles. The workflow generalises its essay framework to substantive nonfiction; preserve the user's requested genre, voice, length, and degree of intervention.

## Establish reader value

Before drafting or restructuring, identify from the brief and source material:

- **Reader:** who will read this, what they already understand, and what matters to them.
- **Status quo:** their plausible current belief, practice, or unanswered question.
- **Problem:** what is wrong, incomplete, or unresolved, and the cost of leaving it that way or benefit of resolving it.
- **Point:** the central claim or answer that changes their understanding. A subject such as “remote work” is not yet a Point.

For persuasive writing, try inverting the Point to discover the belief it challenges. State that belief fairly enough that a reader who holds it would recognise it, and concede what it gets right. Treat the inversion as a diagnostic, not evidence that readers actually believe it. For an explanation, the Problem can be a real gap in understanding; an opposing camp is unnecessary. (PDF p. 1.)

Use supplied evidence to determine how strong the Point can be. Preserve factual qualifications, citations, and the author's intended meaning. Ask for missing information only when it materially changes the argument; do not manufacture stakes, consensus, quotations, or supporting facts to complete the framework.

## Shape the whole piece

Build the introduction around the reader's Problem. Where useful, move through shared context, the status quo, a fair concession, the condition that unsettles it, its consequence, and the proposed resolution. These are rhetorical moves, not mandatory headings or a fixed sentence count. Keep only context that makes the Problem intelligible. (PDF p. 1.)

A surprising fact, quotation, or anecdote earns its opening position when it makes that Problem vivid. A broad historical preamble or an announcement that a topic has received little attention still needs to establish why this reader should care.

Give the title a clear subject and an honest promise. Curiosity can come from a relevant question, an unresolved sequence of events, a violated expectation, or a gap between what the reader and others know. Deliver on that curiosity in the piece.

Where the format calls for an ending, resolve the opening Problem to the extent the evidence permits. Show what the resolution changes, enables, or leaves open. A broader implication should follow from the argument; repeating the opening claim adds little.

## Build units that keep their promises

Apply **Index → Discussion** at the scale the task needs: whole piece, section, paragraph. The Index is the opening that establishes what a unit is doing and introduces its core concepts, or **Key Terms**. The Discussion develops that promise through reasoning, evidence, examples, or consequences. A paragraph's Index is often one sentence; use two or three for a complex setup, opposition, or question. Larger units can need more space. (PDF p. 2.)

Give each section a Point that both governs its own paragraphs and supports the whole piece's Point. Outline claims and their relationships, rather than merely listing subjects. Move, cut, or separately introduce material that does not fulfil its unit's promise.

Put the Point at the end of the Index by default: early, once the reader has the setup needed to understand it. Use **Point-last** deliberately when the reader needs the Discussion before accepting or understanding the claim. In that case, the Index must still supply the question and themes; place the answer at the end of the Discussion. Rescue claims buried in the middle. (PDF p. 3.)

State a unit's Point once, at the chosen location. If a longer piece needs a closing synthesis, use it to draw out consequences or integrate findings instead of repeating the same claim at both ends.

## Make paragraphs cohere

Thread the Index's Key Terms through its Discussion. Use repetition, clear pronouns, and accurate synonyms to maintain recognisable concepts; preserve exact technical names where variation could change the meaning. Each sentence should develop a thread or introduce a clearly connected one. Coherence is conceptual continuity, not mechanically repeating every keyword. (PDF p. 3.)

Within a clause, the **Topic** is the starting point the reader recognises; the **Comment** adds what the clause says about it. Prefer a progression from established information to new information. When a paragraph feels choppy, drifts, or contains several competing relationships, read [paragraph patterns](references/paragraph-patterns.md) and choose a structure that fits its reasoning. (PDF p. 4.)

## Revise and finish

Check from large to small, repairing failures before polishing sentences:

- **Value:** can the intended reader identify the Problem, its stakes, and the Point?
- **Promise:** does the body deliver what the title and introduction lead them to expect?
- **Structure:** does each section advance the argument, with evidence sufficient for its claim?
- **Skim:** read only the Index of each paragraph. Together, these openings should expose the argument's progression. For deliberate Point-last units, they should expose the question and route to its answer. (PDF p. 2, “The Skim Test”.)
- **Flow:** can the reader follow Key Terms and sentence Topics without guessing at references or making unexplained jumps?
- **Economy:** does each passage add reasoning, evidence, orientation, or a useful consequence? Cut repetition that adds none of these; retain repetition that maintains coherence.

For drafting or rewriting, return the requested writing. For critique, identify consequential failures, cite the relevant passage, and propose a concrete repair. Keep the framework's labels and working analysis out of the deliverable unless requested. Scale the process to the task: a short explanation may need one clear paragraph, with no title, elaborate introduction, or conclusion.
